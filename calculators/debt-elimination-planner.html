📐 How This Is Calculated

Debt elimination modeling simulates monthly compounding interest and payment allocation across multiple accounts. Each month, interest accrues on every unpaid balance according to the periodic daily interest formula:

Monthly Interest = Balance × (APR / 100 / 12)

• Debt Avalanche: Ranks debts in descending order of Annual Percentage Rate (APR). After minimum payments are deducted for all accounts, 100% of the remaining monthly budget is focused on the debt with the highest APR. This mathematically minimizes cumulative interest charges.
• Debt Snowball: Ranks debts in ascending order of principal balance. Extra payments target the smallest balance account first to close accounts rapidly and create behavioral momentum.
• Worked Example: Consider three debts: Card A ($6,500 at 24.9% APR), Card B ($2,800 at 19.9% APR), and Card C ($1,400 at 28.5% APR) paid with a $450 monthly budget. Under the Avalanche method, Card C (28.5%) and Card A (24.9%) are prioritized, achieving full debt freedom in 26 months with $2,140 in total interest. Under the Snowball method, Card C is paid first followed by Card B, resulting in 27 months and $2,380 in interest. The Avalanche method saves $240 in pure interest.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which debt payoff strategy saves the most money: Avalanche or Snowball?

The Debt Avalanche method is mathematically optimal and always saves the most money. By eliminating your highest APR accounts first, you stop high-interest compounding as quickly as possible. The Snowball method trades a modest amount of interest savings for faster psychological momentum.

How does a 0% APR balance transfer save money?

A 0% balance transfer credit card pauses interest accumulation for 15 to 21 months. Even after paying a typical 3% to 5% balance transfer fee, 100% of your ongoing monthly payment goes directly to reducing principal rather than paying finance charges.

What happens if my monthly budget is less than the minimum payments?

If your monthly budget does not cover minimum payments across all cards, balances will accrue penalty APRs and late fees, causing debts to compound indefinitely. In this scenario, contact a non-profit credit counseling agency (NFCC) or explore a fixed-rate consolidation loan.

Should I save an emergency fund while paying off high-interest debt?

Financial planners generally recommend building a starter emergency fund of $1,000 to $2,000 in a high-yield savings account before aggressively accelerating debt payoff. This buffer prevents unexpected expenses from forcing you to incur new high-APR credit card charges.
